## Why Shopify Returns Are a Growing Cost Problem

Returns are not a rounding error for most apparel and footwear stores. Ecommerce return rates run well above brick-and-mortar retail, and apparel and footwear categories sit at the high end of that range, with reported rates commonly landing between 25% and 40% depending on category and season ([Ringly](https://www.ringly.io/blog/ecommerce-return-statistics-2026)). Every returned order also carries a real cost: estimates for the fully loaded cost of processing a single return, including reverse shipping, inspection, and restocking, commonly range from $10 to $65 per unit ([Ringly](https://www.ringly.io/blog/ecommerce-return-statistics-2026)).

The gap between online and in-store return rates is not random. Shoppers cannot try on a garment or test a fit before buying online, so they buy on a guess and let the return process correct the mistake. That guess is expensive at scale.

## Sizing and Fit Are the Leading Cause

Across multiple retail return studies, sizing and fit issues are consistently cited as the single leading cause of apparel returns, ahead of damage, late delivery, or a shopper simply changing their mind ([Ringly](https://www.ringly.io/blog/ecommerce-return-statistics-2026)). A shoe that runs half a size small, or a jacket with an unclear fit description, sends the shopper straight to the returns page instead of the closet.

This matters for how you fix the problem. Discount codes and free return shipping treat the symptom. They do nothing to stop the wrong item from being ordered in the first place.

## Bracketing Makes the Problem Worse

Bracketing is the practice of ordering multiple sizes or colors of the same item, planning from the start to return whatever does not fit. It has become common practice among online apparel shoppers, a sharp rise from a decade ago, and it directly inflates return rates for any store that does not address sizing uncertainty upfront ([Ringly](https://www.ringly.io/blog/ecommerce-return-statistics-2026)).

Bracketing is a rational response to uncertainty, not a bad-faith move. A shopper who has been burned by inconsistent sizing on past orders brackets to protect themselves. Reducing that uncertainty at the point of purchase reduces the incentive to bracket at all.

## How a Product Quiz Prevents Returns Before Checkout

A pre-purchase quiz asks the questions a fitting room or a sales associate would ask: body measurements, fit preference, use case, prior brand sizing experience. It routes each shopper to the specific size, cut, or product variant most likely to fit, before the order is placed.

This flips the sequence that causes most returns. Instead of "order, discover the mistake, return, reorder," the shopper answers 4 to 6 questions and orders the right item once. The [quiz design guide](/blog/shopify-product-recommendation-quiz/) covers question count, flow length, and result formatting in detail.

### Fit-Focused Quiz Questions That Work

| Question | What it prevents |
| --- | --- |
| "What's your usual size in [comparable brand]?" | Cross-brand sizing mismatches |
| "Do you prefer a snug, regular, or relaxed fit?" | Wrong fit style for the shopper's preference |
| "What will you primarily use this for?" | Wrong product line for the use case |
| "Have you had sizing issues with us before?" | Repeat mis-sizing for returning customers |

## Returns Reduction and AOV Gains Work Together

Fixing sizing uncertainty does not just cut returns. It also changes what the shopper adds to cart. Recommendation-driven flows lift average order value 20 to 25% in typical deployments, since a confident shopper is more willing to add a second item or a higher-tier option than one who is unsure the first item will even fit ([Clerk.io](https://www.clerk.io/blog/product-recommendations-statistics)). A returns-focused quiz and an upsell-focused quiz are not competing goals. The same fit-confidence signal drives both outcomes.
